Here is a prediction. Within a few years, France will be forced to do what no French government dares to propose. The only question is who gives the order: Paris, or its creditors.

This is not a mood. It is arithmetic. In 2024, France borrowed about 170 billion euros. Its economy grew by about 95 billion [F027?]. Read that again: each euro of new debt bought fifty-five cents of new economy. A business run this way goes bankrupt. A country run this way goes to Brussels, or to the IMF, or to its bondholders on its knees.

The numbers are not hiding. Interest is now the fastest-growing item in the French budget — 65 billion euros in 2026, 74 billion in 2027, on its way to 124 billion by 2030 if nothing changes [F024][F025]. That last number is more than the budget of the entire French school system. In the autumn of 2025, France lost its double-A credit rating; two agencies cut it within five weeks [F026]. More than half of French government debt now sits in foreign hands — 54.6 percent and rising [F028]. France must raise roughly six billion euros from those hands every week, just to stay where it is [F029].

I want to be honest about what this prediction is not. It is not a forecast of missed coupons on a specific date; rich countries in the euro rarely default outright — they get "programs," which is default with better manners and someone else holding the pen. Greece kept the euro and lost its budget for a decade. Nor is it a claim that the markets are panicking: they are not, and in 2025 France actually borrowed slightly cheaper than in 2024 [F029]. The European Central Bank stands behind French bonds — but its backstop is conditional on exactly the fiscal adjustment France keeps refusing. The trap is not a rate spiral. It is a volume problem: a debt stock so large [F002] that even calm interest rates eat the budget alive, one refinanced bond at a time.

So the countdown is real, but it counts down to a choice, not a coupon. Either France redesigns the machine on its own terms, or the redesign will be handed to it — faster, dumber, and crueler than anything a French parliament would write. This essay is about the machine, why the French will not fix it voluntarily, and what fixing it would actually take.

## 1. How France got poorer without noticing

Within living memory, France and America were nearly even. In 2000, the average French person produced about a quarter less than the average American; by 2023 the gap had grown to roughly a third [F006?]. Measurements differ on the exact size — they agree completely on the direction. Germany slipped too, but less. Italy slipped more. France sits in the middle of a sinking continent, sinking.

For decades, the French had a comeback, and it was a good one: *we work less, but better*. It was true. In 2000, French workers produced more per hour than Americans. The comeback is now dead. The Banque de France — not a think tank with an agenda, the country's own central bank — decomposed the gap: most of it still comes from hours not worked, but more than a third now comes from producing less per hour worked. In 2000, that second gap did not exist [F007].

Hold both halves in your head, because every French debate about growth amputates one of them.

The first half: France works very little. Not the employed French — a French worker puts in under 1,500 hours a year, about the same as a German [F008]. The difference is how many French people work at all, and for how much of their lives. They start late. They leave early: barely six in ten French people aged 55 to 64 hold a job, against roughly three-quarters in Germany and Sweden [F011?]. Add it up and France supplies among the fewest hours of work per inhabitant in the developed world [F012?].

The second half: the productivity engine has stalled. French output per hour has slipped roughly 3 to 5 percent below its own pre-2019 trend [F014?], and the long game looks worse. France spends 2.2 percent of GDP on research and development — the same as it did in 2014, and in 2000. Germany spends 3.1 percent. South Korea spends 5 [F009]. Mario Draghi's report to the European Commission named the disease: Europe is caught in a "middle technology trap," its corporate champions still perfecting the automobile while America's moved to software, then to AI [F010]. France is caught in the European trap while lagging even the European average.

One gap could be a choice. Two gaps, compounding, funded by borrowing — that is not a lifestyle. That is a liquidation.

## 2. The usual suspects are innocent

Every French election litigates the same three explanations. None survives contact with the numbers.

*"The state is starved."* France spends 57 percent of GDP through its public sector — the highest share in the OECD, tied with Finland, nine points above the euro-area average [F001][F005]. It taxes 43 to 44 percent of GDP [F004]. If public money produced growth by volume, France would be Singapore. Whatever is wrong, it is not a lack of fuel.

*"Austerity is bleeding us."* France has not balanced a budget since 1974. Its mildest deficit of the past seven years was 2.3 percent of GDP; it now runs above 5 [F003]. There has been no austerity. There has been its opposite: a state borrowing five points of GDP a year to buy one point of growth.

*"It's Europe's fault — fix Brussels first."* Half true, and the true half changes nothing. Yes, the productivity gap is continental [F010], and yes, Europe needs capital-markets scale France cannot build alone. But the hours-per-capita gap is French [F012?], the pension arithmetic is French [F018], and the R&D stagnation is French — France trails not just America but the EU average trend [F009]. A French government serious about growth has a decade of purely domestic work before Brussels becomes the binding constraint.

And one suspect is not merely innocent but framed: the French themselves. France is not short of talent or appeal. It has ranked first in Europe for foreign investment projects for years running [F019?]. When policy moved — labor-law reform in 2017, cheaper apprenticeships, a flat tax on capital — unemployment fell from 10.5 percent to about 7.5, a level France had not seen in four decades [F020?]. The raw material responds. Something else is wasting it.

## 3. The trade nobody named

Follow the money and the French model stops being mysterious. France does not out-spend its neighbors on schools, or courts, or soldiers, or science. Of the nine-point spending gap with the euro area, two-thirds is social protection [F005]. And the heart of social protection is one machine: pensions.

France spends almost 14 percent of its GDP on public pensions — 388 billion euros a year, four full points of GDP more than Germany [F018]. Here is what that buys, and it is worth saying without sneering, because it is in one sense a magnificent achievement: French retirees live as well as working-age French people, with a *lower* poverty rate. In Germany, in most of the OECD, retirement means living slightly below the workers who fund you. In France it does not [F018].

Now assemble the whole trade. A French life starts work late, works under 1,500 hours a year [F008], exits before sixty-three, and then draws two decades of pension at the living standard of a worker [F018]. The gap between what this costs and what the economy produces is closed with borrowed money — five points of GDP a year [F003], more than half of it lent by foreigners [F028]. And the future that is supposed to repay the loan is being starved too: the R&D budget of the future has been flat for twenty-five years [F009], and French fifteen-year-olds just posted one of the sharpest math declines in the rich world [F022?].

This is the transfer from the future to the present. No party proposed it. No one voted on the sum. It assembled itself, one generous and defensible decision at a time, over fifty years — and each decision came with a constituency that will defend it to the death, which is precisely why the sum is indefensible and untouchable at once.

Two anesthetics kept the trade painless. Cheap credit was the first: for a decade, borrowing cost nothing, so the gap between the model's cost and the economy's output felt free. The productivity edge was the second: as long as the French produced more per hour than everyone else, working fewer hours looked like efficiency, not decline. Both anesthetics have now worn off [F007][F024]. What remains is the pain, on schedule.

## 4. What France believes

Here is where most essays reach for policy. Policy is the easy part. The hard part is that the French, as a people, have talked themselves out of the system that pays for their model.

The numbers are stark. Sixty-two percent of the French hold a negative view of capitalism — a number stable across booms, busts, and pandemics [F021]. In a 34-country survey, France sits deep in the anti-capitalist camp; the criticism ranked first by French respondents is that capitalism "leads to growing inequality" [F021]. This is not universal grumbling about elites. Poles, Americans, Czechs, Japanese, Koreans — and Swedes, once you avoid the trigger-word "capitalism" — lean the other way [F021].

I will not pretend this belief is stupid. A system is judged by its story, and France's growth story for half a century has been: profits went up, factories went east, and the suburbs got poorer. The French did not learn anti-capitalism from Marxist professors — well, not only. They learned it from watching a version of capitalism that kept its gains offshore, while the state visibly paid for everything they loved.

But the belief has consequences, and the consequences compound. A public that despises markets will not let its savings near them: the French save 17 to 18 percent of their income — a rate Americans would envy — and then park it in state-guaranteed booklets and life-insurance funds that lend it back to the government [F023?]. The world's most anti-capitalist rich country has, in effect, chosen to fund its state's deficits instead of its companies' growth. The circle is perfect: no equity, so firms stay small or sell to Americans; no growth, so the state compensates with transfers; more transfers, more taxes, more debt; and the worse the outcome, the more capitalism gets the blame.

Now, the honest counter-argument, because it is strong: France *did* reform, recently, and reforms *worked*. Labor law in 2017. Apprenticeships. The flat tax. Unemployment fell three points [F020?]. This proves the French mindset does not make reform impossible — and any thesis claiming so is dead on arrival. What the mindset does is subtler and in a way worse: it caps the dose and voids the warranty. Each reform passes at half-strength, against the street; and none is ever safe — the 2023 pension reform, the single most load-bearing fiscal decision of the decade, was suspended two years later to buy one parliamentary season of survival [F026]. Investors can price a country that refuses to reform. They cannot price a country that reforms and un-reforms on a two-year cycle. That is what the double-A downgrades of 2025 actually said [F026].

So the belief is the bottleneck. Not because it blocks change absolutely, but because it makes every change small, slow, and reversible — in a decade when the arithmetic demands large, fast, and permanent.

## 5. The hand France holds

If France were a poor bet, this essay would be a eulogy. It is the opposite of a poor bet, which is what makes the waste enraging.

Start with power, in the literal sense. France generates about two-thirds of its electricity from nuclear plants [F017?] — the cheapest low-carbon baseload of any large economy, at the exact moment in history when the next industrial revolution, AI, is constrained by exactly one input: reliable clean electricity. Germany spent twenty years dismantling the same asset France kept. The single most valuable industrial endowment in Europe for the 2030s is already bolted into French soil.

Add capital: those same anti-capitalist households sit on one of Europe's deepest savings pools [F023?] — misallocated, not missing. Add people: France still mints elite engineers and mathematicians that Silicon Valley and its own AI labs fight over, and its demographics, while aging, decay slower than Germany's or Italy's. Add proof of demand: when the world's money looks for a European home, it already picks France first [F019?].

Assets, capital, talent, demand. What is missing is not an endowment. It is an operating system that turns endowments into output — and a country that wants it to.

## 6. The program: five reversals

What follows is a vision, not a manifesto for the next election. Every item breaks a French taboo; that is a feature. Feasibility is a variable, not a constraint — the whole point of the countdown is that "infeasible" is a deadline away from becoming "inevitable."

**First reversal: more of life at work.** The goal is brutal and simple: raise hours worked per inhabitant to northern-European levels [F012?]. That means the effective retirement age converges to Germany's, not by parametric nudges suspended at the first strike [F026], but by a systemic redesign — a universal points system where working longer always pays and the exit age follows life expectancy automatically, outside annual politics. It means attacking the poverty traps and marginal tax cliffs that make extra hours worthless at the bottom [F015], and it means seniors: a country where barely 60 percent of 55-to-64-year-olds work [F011?] is not "protecting" them — it is writing off its most experienced decade of labor. The precedent exists: nobody believed the French labor market could bend, then it bent three points [F020?].

**Second reversal: savings into risk.** Take the machine that funnels French savings to the French deficit [F023?] and point it at French equity. A national auto-enrollment pension fund — capitalization layered on top of the pay-as-you-go core, as Sweden built after its crisis [X01] — does three things at once: it gives every French worker a personal stake in capitalism's success (watch the polls [F021] move when ownership does), it builds the domestic equity base that keeps French firms French, and it slowly weans the pension system off the demographic cliff. Alongside: kill the production taxes that tax firms before they earn a euro of profit — several times the German level [F016?] — and make simplification a budgeted policy with a number on it, not a slogan.

**Third reversal: the frontier, on purpose.** France cannot subsidize its way to 3 percent R&D intensity [F009]; it must make private research pay. That means universities freed to compete for talent and money; a research tax credit rebuilt around young firms instead of incumbent optimization [Q05]; and one deliberate, Draghi-scale bet [F010]: France as the place where Europe's AI runs — because it is the only large economy that can power it [F017?]. Energy sovereignty plus compute plus mathematicians is an actual national strategy. It is sitting on the table, unclaimed.

**Fourth reversal: a state that protects people, not positions.** The Swedish crisis of 1991 forced Stockholm to cut public spending by twenty points of GDP over fifteen years — and Sweden emerged with its welfare state intact and its growth restored [X01]. The French translation is not "austerity"; it is composition. Stabilizing the debt requires about 25 billion euros of adjustment a year for a five-year term [F025] — roughly one percent of GDP annually, painful but human-scale — *if* it starts now and *if* it comes from the transfer machine [F005][F018] rather than from schools, courts, and labs, which are not what makes France expensive. Pair it with the Danish deal [X03]: let firms fire, and protect workers with real money and real retraining. Protect the person, not the post. The state France needs is not smaller everywhere; it is smaller where it buys the present and bigger where it builds the future.

**Fifth reversal: say the trade out loud.** This is the reform that makes the others possible. For fifty years, every French government has sold reform as a technical fix imposed by others — Brussels demands it, the ratings require it, the markets insist. The result is a nation that experiences its own survival as humiliation, and repeals it at the first chance [F026]. The alternative is to name the actual deal: *France has been buying today with tomorrow's money; tomorrow has arrived; here is how we keep what matters.* Countries can change their minds — Sweden's did after 1991 [X01], and the French mind is not uniform: the young entrepreneurs crowding Station F did not get the memo that their country despises what they do [F019?]. Beliefs follow ownership and lived proof [F021]. Give the French a stake in growth, and the growth story stops being someone else's.

## 7. Why anything would change

The fair question: if the French mindset caps every reform, why would any of this happen?

Because of the countdown. Sweden did not choose its 1991 program out of philosophical maturity; the krona broke, rates hit 500 percent, and the money ran out [X01]. Greece did not choose at all — the pen was held in Brussels and Washington. Crises come to over-borrowed welfare states the way winter comes; the only national choice is whether the plan exists before the storm, or whether the storm writes the plan.

That is the real function of naming the countdown now. Watch the markers, they are public: the interest line crossing each ministry's budget one by one [F024][F025]; the debt ratio grinding past 120 percent [F025]; the foreign-held share of the debt climbing [F028]; each rating notch [F026]; each reform passed and un-passed [F026]. None of these is the crisis. Each one moves the date on which some French government — probably not one anyone would choose — finds the road closed, the creditors seated, and the choices pre-made.

France still holds the pen. It holds assets any nation would envy, a demonstrated (if grudging) capacity to reform, and a couple of years of road. The window between "too comfortable to change" and "too broke to choose" is narrow, and France is inside it now.

The trade was never named, so it was never chosen. Name it, and France can finally choose — the first real choice it has faced in fifty years: keep pretending the future will pay, until the future sends its collectors; or work more, own more, risk more, and make the French model something no one has to lie about to defend.

The prediction stands. The forced hour is coming. The only open question — the only one that ever mattered — is whose hand is on the pen.

# When France Runs Out of Road

In 2025, France borrowed 152 billion euros. Its economy grew by 56 billion [F027].

Read the two numbers again. They are the whole essay. Almost three euros of new debt for every euro of new economy. The year before, one euro seventy [F027]. A household living this way would cut up its cards. A firm would be in receivership by spring. A country is bigger and slower than either, so a country gets years of warnings instead. Then one morning the warnings stop, and someone else is writing its budget.

I think that morning is coming for France, and sooner than anyone in government is prepared to say out loud. I will not pretend to know the year. Economists who predict dates end up as punchlines, and French debt has been declared doomed before by people who are now retired. What I can do is show you the road, the speed, and the strangest part: why the brakes cannot be pulled. The hands that could pull them belong to the passengers the speed is serving. Then I want to convince you of something stranger still. This forced, unwanted moment is also the best chance France has had in fifty years to become what it keeps claiming it already is. The strongest economy in Europe. Not a museum with good trains.

## Where I stand

You should know who is talking, because parts of this essay will sound like they were written by a man in a pinstripe suit, and they were not.

My politics are of the left. I believe a country where you can die because you are poor has failed at something basic. I believe the strong owe the weak more than pity. I think French healthcare, which asks what is wrong with you before it asks how you will pay, is one of the finest things my country has built. I would defend it against the American alternative without a second of hesitation. The United States spends seventeen percent of its economy on health, nearly six points more than France, and dies five years younger [F055]. Its poorest men die fourteen years before its richest [F054]. Nobody sane looks at that and calls it the model.

I am writing this essay because of those convictions, not despite them. The French social model is a conquest. It is also, on today's numbers, a conquest paid for by people who cannot vote yet. And the first people to lose it, when the money runs out, will be exactly the people it exists to protect. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is what happened in Greece, and we will get there. If you are on the left and the last three sentences made you flinch, this essay was written for you. If you are on the right and you are already nodding along, be patient. Some of what follows will break your story too.

## The wall

Start with the bill that is already printed.

In 2026, France will pay about 65 billion euros of interest on its debt. In 2027, about 74 billion [F024]. If nothing changes, the number reaches roughly 124 billion by 2030, which would make interest nearly twice the defense budget [F025]. Not repayment. Just interest, the cost of standing still.

The stock behind that bill is 3,460 billion euros and rising, nearly 116 percent of GDP [F002][F027]. More than half of it, 54.6 percent, is held by foreigners [F028], in a currency France cannot print. So the French treasury must raise six billion euros a week, every week of the year, just to keep the machine running [F029]. In the autumn of 2025, two rating agencies took away France's double-A within five weeks of each other [F026]. The second one, S&P, moved outside its own calendar. Its explanation fits in one line: the agency doubted that France's political system could still deliver a budget.

Now, honesty, because doom-mongering about French debt is a genre older than I am. There is no panic in the market. France borrowed slightly cheaper in 2025 than in 2024 [F029]. The spread over German bonds sits around 80 basis points, elevated but far from crisis levels [F026]. And the European Central Bank stands somewhere behind it all, which is why many sensible people in the government and around it will tell you that nothing can really happen.

They are half right, and the half matters. Rich countries in the euro do not default the way emerging markets default. What they get instead is called a program. Greece kept the euro through its crisis and lost its budget for a decade; the pen that wrote Greek fiscal policy was held in Brussels and Washington. A program is a default with better manners. And the ECB's protection has a condition attached, spelled out in its own rules: it shields countries that are doing their fiscal adjustment. It does not replace the adjustment. So the trap in front of France is not a spiral of interest rates. Rates could stay calm for years. The trap is volume: a stock so large that even polite interest rates eat a ministry's worth of budget every parliament, one refinanced bond at a time.

If you want to see the early, polite form of what that ends in, it already exists. Since July 2024 France has been under the EU's excessive deficit procedure, its budgets bound to a seven-year expenditure path negotiated with Brussels and checked every spring [F073]. The pen has not changed hands. But another hand is now resting on it.

Which means the question was never whether France adjusts. The question is who holds the pen.

## The road here

France has been level with America before. Around 1980, at the end of the thirty-year boom the French still call *les trente glorieuses*, French GDP per person briefly matched the American figure in the dollars of the day [F069]. Adjusted for what money actually bought, it stood at about four fifths of the US level. No large European country ever came closer. That is the world my parents' generation grew up inside, and it explains something about France that outsiders miss: the country is not arrogant about its rank out of fantasy. It remembers.

Since then the slide has run one way. A quarter behind America in 2000, a third behind today, on the Banque de France's accounting [F006]. Distrust that number if you like. GDP per person is an average, and America's average rides on a very top-heavy distribution. Measured median to median, the gap shrinks to roughly a quarter [F076]. Fine. The direction is not in dispute, and the size was never the interesting question. The interesting question is what the gap is made of, because that decides whether it can be closed. And the parts, unlike the average, have nothing to do with American billionaires.

The Banque de France took it apart [F007]. It is made of two things.

The first is hours. France supplies 673 hours of work per inhabitant per year; Germany supplies 776 [F031]. And before anyone reaches for the cliché: this is not about lazy workers. An employed French person works about as many hours as an employed German [F008], and a French hour remains among the most productive in the world [F053]. The missing hours sit at the two edges of working life. The French start late, and they leave early. Barely six in ten people aged 55 to 64 hold a job; in Germany it is three in four, in Sweden nearly four in five [F011]. Past 60, the gap is all there is [F011]. Add up the edges and France is missing the equivalent of 2.8 million full careers [F031].

Why should you care about a statistic like that? Because of what a career is in France. A career is a payer. French pensions run on répartition: today's workers pay today's retirees, directly, euro for euro. Every missing career is a missing payer in a machine that is now short five points of GDP a year [F003]. The hours gap is not a lifestyle detail. It is the deficit, seen from upstream.

For decades France had a beautiful answer to all of this: *we work less, but better.* And it was true. In 2000, a French hour of work produced more than an American one [F007]. Fewer hours at higher quality: the gap was a choice, and an honorable one. Less income, more August.

But the answer died, and this is the fact the whole essay turns on. Producing less per hour now explains more than a third of the gap with America, and that share is growing; in 2000 it did not exist [F007]. The engine that paid for the leisure is stalling, and you can see the stall wherever you look for the future. Research spending has been frozen at 2.2 percent of GDP since 2000, while Germany climbed to 3.1 and South Korea to 5 [F009]. Draghi's report to the European Commission named the trap: Europe still perfecting the car while America moved to software, then to AI [F010]. And upstream of everything, French fifteen-year-olds just posted the sharpest math decline ever recorded in the national series. They now test exactly average for the OECD [F022]. Average. The country of Pascal.

So hold the two facts together, because French politics never does. A country that worked less *while out-producing everyone per hour* was making a trade. A country that keeps the leisure after losing the edge, and covers the difference with three euros of debt per euro of growth, is not trading anymore. It is liquidating.

## The usual suspects

Every French election tries the same three defendants. The evidence acquits them all.

*The state is starved.* France's public sector spends 57 percent of GDP, the highest share in the OECD alongside Finland, nine points above the euro-area average [F001][F005]. It taxes 43 to 44 percent [F004]. If public money produced prosperity by volume, France would have no rival on earth.

*Austerity is bleeding us.* France has not balanced a budget since 1974. Its mildest deficit in the past seven years was 2.3 percent of GDP; the current one is above 5 [F003]. Borrowing five points of GDP a year to obtain one point of growth has a name, and the name is not austerity.

*It's Europe's fault.* Half true, and the true half changes nothing. Yes, the productivity trap is continental [F010], and Europe lacks the capital markets to scale its own champions. But the hours gap is French [F031]. The pension arithmetic is French [F018]. The R&D freeze is French [F009]. The schools are French. The French government has a decade of purely domestic work in front of it before Brussels becomes its binding constraint.

And one suspect deserves an explicit acquittal: the French themselves. Foreign investors have picked France as their top European destination for years running [F019]. American AI labs raid French math departments [F045]. When policy actually moved, between 2015 and 2023, on labor law and apprenticeships, unemployment fell three points, to lows France had not seen in forty years [F020]. It has drifted back up since the moving stopped [F020]. The raw material responds like any other. Something upstream keeps wasting it.

So follow the money.

## The trade nobody named

Where do 57 points of GDP go? Not where you would guess. France does not out-spend its neighbors on schools, courts, police or science. On courts it is starved: eleven judges per hundred thousand people, half the European Union's median [F049]. Two thirds of the entire spending gap with the euro area is one category: social protection [F005]. And at the center of social protection sits one machine. Pensions: 407 billion euros in 2024, 13.9 percent of GDP, four full points more than Germany [F018]. Twice what France spends on its entire education system, from kindergarten to the doctorate [F051]. Count public money alone and the ratio passes two and a half [F051].

What does the machine buy? Something genuinely rare. In cash terms, French retirees live exactly as well as everyone else; their poverty rate runs a third below the national one, and at less than half the OECD average [F077]. Say it plainly, because the rest of this essay will sound like an attack on it: keeping old age out of poverty is a triumph of civilization.

Now look closer, because the machine sailed past poverty prevention a long time ago. Count the homes retirees own outright, as any economist would, and they live as well as the workers who fund them, five to nine points above the national average [F077]. They are also the cohort that owns the country. Households between fifty and eighty hold sixty-one percent of French wealth; the average retired household is worth twenty-nine percent more than the average working one [F078]. And the pensions exceed what their recipients consume: retirees, as a group, save eight percent of their income [F078]. Sit with that sentence. A transfer designed to fund consumption in old age is, at the margin, being banked. The banked surplus eventually becomes inheritance, and the average French heir is fifty years old [F079]. So wealth in France flows from the old to the almost-old, and the thirty-year-old renter is taxed so that the seventy-year-old homeowner can keep saving. None of this dishonors retirees. They were promised it, and they paid for their own elders in their day. But a pension exists so that stopping work does not mean falling into poverty. Parity with the working would already be generous. What France built goes past parity, into something its politics has never named.

But run the arithmetic of the machine. To pay pensions at that level out of current wages, you need one of three things: more people working, each worker producing more, or someone else's money. France has fewer people at work than almost any of its peers, we established that. Its productivity edge is gone, we established that too. Which leaves the third option, and the third option is exactly what the deficit is [F003]. The machine's books balance on the credit card.

For a generation, two anesthetics made this painless. Credit cost nothing, so the gap between what the model cost and what the economy produced felt free. And the productivity edge, while it lasted, made the whole arrangement look earned. Both anesthetics are now gone [F024][F007]. Therefore the pain arrives, on schedule, which is what the wall in section two actually is.

And while the machine pays for the present, look at what it does to the people who will inherit it. A French twenty-five-year-old enters work later than almost any European. She pays one of the developed world's heaviest tax wedges [F015] to fund retirees who live better than she does [F018]. Her schooling was among the most class-determined in the OECD [F047]. Housing eats more of her income than it ate of her parents' at her age [F042]. Whatever family wealth exists will reach her around fifty, when her own career is nearly run [F079]. Her inheritance at birth is a debt above 115 percent of GDP that bought nothing she will use [F002], plus a pension promise the projections quietly erode [F041]. The most talented of her generation can read this table. They are leaving, and American laboratories are delighted [F045].

Nobody designed this. Nobody voted for it. It assembled itself, one generous and defensible decision at a time, over fifty years, and every single piece has a constituency that will fight for it. Fine, you say: democracies correct course. Painfully, late, but they correct. Here is the part of the French situation I find genuinely frightening: this one cannot.

## The impasse

France did try. In 2023 the government passed the most consequential fiscal reform of the decade, raising the retirement age. In 2025 a successor government suspended it, trading the reform away to survive a no-confidence vote [F026]. Pass at half strength, then un-pass: 1995, 2010, 2019, 2023. Four governments of three different political families have now attempted the same repair and met the same wall. When the identical failure repeats across decades and parties, stop blaming courage. Look at the wiring.

The wiring is arithmetic. The French electorate is old, and turnout makes it older. Among registered voters under 35, one in four did not vote at all in 2022; among the 50-to-69, fewer than one in ten [F070]. At the legislative elections, the ones that decide pension law, 28 percent of under-30s voted, against 59 percent of the over-65s [F070]. Weight the electorate by those numbers and you reach the conclusion that decides everything downstream. At the 2022 legislative elections, voters aged 55 and over cast a majority of all the ballots, about 53 percent [F070]. When turnout surged in 2024, their share still held near half. They are the people the transfer machine serves, and their weight grows every single year [F038]. The people who would pay for the repair are a shrinking electoral minority. The people the machine pays are the electorate.

So the impasse is not a metaphor. It is a wiring diagram. On pensions, French democracy is structurally incapable of representing the people the decision most concerns, because they are twenty-five, or five, or not born. And before you object that this is every democracy's problem: it is not. In 2021, Germany's constitutional court struck down a federal law for, in its own words, "unilaterally offloading burdens onto the future" [F071]. The judges ruled that fundamental rights are "intertemporal guarantees of freedom." They ruled that the state owes protection to people who "do not yet carry any fundamental rights." And Germany's constitution backs them with a debt brake written into its text. The unborn have standing in Karlsruhe. In France, the closest equivalent covers environmental harm, narrowly, and fiscal matters not at all [F071]. The French Republic is wired to hear only the living, and among the living, mostly the old.

If the wiring sounds like a theory, read the tax code, where it leaves fingerprints. French wage earners deduct ten percent of their income for professional expenses. French pensioners deduct ten percent too, for professional expenses they do not have. When parliament invented that deduction in 1977, the budget minister objected, on the record, that pensions carry no professional costs. Parliament voted it anyway [F080]. It now costs some 4.5 billion a year, a third of it flowing to the richest tenth of households [F080]. In 2025 a government finally proposed replacing it with a flat two-thousand-euro allowance. The National Assembly killed the reform, 213 votes to 17 [F080].

Indexation obeys the same wiring. Pensions rise with inflation automatically, by statute; freezing them takes a vote that deputies must own in public [F081]. The tax brackets of the working rise only if a budget passes. In December 2024 both rules fired at once. Parliament censured a government partly for trying to under-index pensions, the first successful censure since 1962 [F083]. The fallen budget meant pensions rose their full 2.2 percent by default, while workers' tax brackets sat frozen until a new budget limped through in February [F081]. And when a president raised social charges on pensions in 2018, having offset them for workers, the rise on retirees was half-repealed within twelve months [F082].

Now the honest complication, because majority governments did trim pensions four times in the 2010s [F084]. The full record supports a sharper law, not a softer one. Touching retirees is survivable for a government that has a majority, and fatal without one [F084]. France has not produced a majority since 2022, the year the electorate described above hung its parliament, and demography makes assemblies where pensioners hold the balance likelier with every cycle [F070][F038]. Every attempt since has died. The freeze was stripped from the budget twice. The deduction reform lost 213 to 17. The indexation delay took the whole government with it.

Take stock of what that means for everything this essay has established so far. The machine overpays the present. The bill compounds at six billion a week. And the only constitutional force that could stop it, the voters, are its beneficiaries. A problem that cannot be voted away has exactly three exits. A crisis that suspends normal politics, the way Sweden's did. A design that pays the older majority to say yes instead of asking it to sacrifice. And a rewiring: write the future into French fundamental law, the way Germany did, so the next machine cannot be built. Keep the three in mind. They are the program's skeleton.

## What France believes

The impasse explains why reforms die in parliament. It does not explain why they die in the street first. That answer is about belief, and it matters because the program at the end of this essay stands or falls on whether beliefs can move.

Sixty-two percent of the French hold a negative view of capitalism, a number stable through booms, crises and pandemics [F021]. In a thirty-four-country survey, pro-market attitudes prevail in six or seven countries; France anchors the other camp [F021]. The belief is not stupid, and it was not taught by philosophy professors. It was learned from fifty years in which growth felt like something that happened offshore. Profits left. Factories closed. And the one institution that visibly paid for everything people loved was the state. If the market never made you an offer, why would you love it?

But look inside the same polls and the belief comes apart in a revealing place: French executives half-accept capitalism; French workers reject it two to one [F021]. The dividing line is not ideology. It is ownership. And ownership is precisely what French savers do not have. Their savings total 6,590 billion euros, twice the national debt [F023]. Under five percent of that is invested directly in listed companies. The rest sleeps in state-guaranteed booklets and life-insurance funds, whose managers buy government bonds. Run the loop once and you will never unsee it. A public this hostile to capitalism lends its savings to the state. The state spends them on the deficit. The deficit pays for the model. And the model compensates for the growth that never comes, because the country's capital never reaches the country's companies. Firms starved of equity stay small or sell to foreigners [F023][F039]. Growth disappoints. The state compensates. Capitalism takes the blame. Tighter every turn.

Can the loop run backward? Two experiments say yes, and they are the hinge of this essay. In England, researchers handed randomly chosen people money to invest in stocks for six weeks [F035]. Their values shifted measurably toward markets, and stayed shifted a year later. Nobody lectured them. Familiarity ate distrust. In Buenos Aires, squatters received legal title to their plots through a near-random allocation. Their beliefs about markets and merit moved twenty percent closer to the general population's than their untitled neighbors', despite identical lives [F036]. Ownership changes what people believe. Not arguments. Ownership. Hold that; it is the engine of the entire second half.

## What growth is for

One more piece of ground to clear, because in France the word "growth" itself is suspect, and some of that suspicion is deserved.

The suspicion says: growth for whom? America grew magnificently while the life expectancy of its poorest stood still [F054]. If that is growth, France is right to refuse it. I agree, without reservation. So let me state the yardstick this essay actually uses, and will use again at the end. A program succeeds if two curves rise together: the living standard of the poorest quarter, measured by what is left after housing and healthcare, and the quality of life of everyone else. Growth is the instrument. It was never the goal.

But here is what the French debate misses about that yardstick: the floor itself runs on growth. The services the left loves most, care and teaching, are precisely the ones that get relatively more expensive as the rest of the economy advances [F068]. Economists call it Baumol's disease. It means a welfare state's costs rise with the frontier, whether the frontier is yours or someone else's. A France that stops growing does not keep its floor at today's level. It keeps the floor's price tag and loses the income that paid it. We are living that exact experiment now, and the results come in every autumn with the budget.

One prejudice on each side deserves a burial before we move on. To the right, which believes pay measures merit: it does not. Prices measure scarcity and scale, which is why water is priceless and nearly free, and why a doctor on regulated tariffs earns less than a man who moves corporate money [F066]. The poor are not worth less; the market was never measuring worth. And to the left, which believes billionaires prove theft: the best estimate we have says innovators capture about two percent of the value they create [F063]. The other ninety-eight percent flows to everyone who uses their work. Confiscating the receipt does not recover the surplus. The floor needs no apology, and neither does the engine. A serious country runs both.

## The hand France holds

If France were a bad bet, this essay would end here as an obituary. Instead, look at the cards.

Electricity, first. France generates about two thirds of its power from nuclear plants [F017], which makes its grid the cleanest of any large economy, at an eighth of Germany's carbon intensity [F064]. For thirty years this looked like an expensive eccentricity. Then artificial intelligence turned electricity into the scarcest industrial input on earth. France woke up holding what every technology company now needs and almost no one in Europe has: abundant, low-carbon, sovereign power. The market has already voted: 109 billion euros of AI infrastructure commitments, the largest datacenter pipeline in Europe, anchored explicitly on the nuclear fleet [F033].

Capital, second: the 6,590 billion sleeping in the wrong accounts [F023]. Talent, third: the mathematicians America keeps hiring [F045]. Demand, fourth: the foreign investment rankings France keeps topping [F019].

But one card is expiring as you read. French fertility has fallen from 2.0 to 1.56 in fifteen years, and the national statistics office now assumes 1.45 [F038]. In 2025, for the first time since 1945, deaths outnumbered births [F044]. The working-age population starts shrinking around 2040 [F038]. France's famous demographic advantage over Germany and Italy is a stock, not a flow, and the stock is running down. So the window in which this hand can be played is open, and it is closing. Which brings us, finally, to what playing it would look like.

## The program

No miracle is required. That is the first thing to understand. What is required is arithmetic plus sequence: seven moves, each feeding the next.

**Work.** The single most powerful lever in France is the effective retirement age [F031]. Aligning French employment with Germany's would add 1.5 million jobs and about 3.2 percent of GDP [F030]. And because every worker is a payer, it would add roughly 38 billion euros a year of public revenue. So the program starts there. One points-based pension system replaces the forty-two existing regimes [F075]. The exit age follows life expectancy automatically, the way Sweden's does, outside the reach of each year's parliamentary panic. Alongside it, an attack on the absurd tax cliffs around the minimum wage, where an extra hour of work can lose seventy cents on the euro [F015]. The 2015-2023 record proves the labor market responds [F020]. This lever pays for the rest.

**The state, rebalanced.** Pensions run four points of GDP above Germany [F018]. That makes them the only envelope large enough to fund everything else. Stabilizing the debt takes about 25 billion a year [F025]. Cutting production taxes to peer levels hands business back 65 to 70 billion more. Today those taxes take 104 billion, triple the European norm, from firms before they earn a cent of profit [F016]. Impossible? Sweden cut public spending from 70 to 51 percent of GDP in fifteen years, and here is the detail France should tattoo on its forearm: health lost 0.1 points and education 0.2. The cuts fell on transfers and subsidies, and the welfare state came out intact [F040]. Composition, not amputation. Meanwhile the parts of the French state that are actually starved, the courts with their eleven judges per hundred thousand [F049], get reinforced, because the point was never a smaller state. It is a state that stops insuring the present so generously that it cannot govern the future. And the repaired rules get written where annual politics cannot reach them; the impasse taught us what happens otherwise. A fiscal sustainability rule and the pension link go to constitutional or organic-law level. The future gets the standing in French law that Karlsruhe already gave it in German law [F071].

**Capital.** Open a national auto-enrollment pension fund. Three to four percent of every wage, invested in equity by default, opt-out allowed. Thirty years of compounding builds a fund worth half of GDP or more. It solves three problems with one instrument. It gives the pension system a funded layer against the demographic slide [F038]. It gives French firms the domestic equity they starve for [F023]. And, remember England and Buenos Aires [F035][F036], it puts a growing account statement in every worker's pocket, which is the only argument for capitalism that has ever moved anyone. One warning from history: France created exactly such a reserve fund in 1999, and a decade later ordered it to hand two billion a year to the deficit instead [F072]. So the new one gets Norwegian-style governance, a statutory mandate no minister can touch.

**The frontier.** Energy-intensive intelligence is France's opening as a great economic power, the first in half a century. Play it deliberately. Six new reactors are already ordered: 83 billion euros in today's money, first unit in 2038 [F032]. Power-hungry compute gets grid priority and land. Research spending rises from 2.2 toward 3.5 percent of GDP, and the increase goes to young firms, not to the tax-credit optimizers who absorbed the last twenty years of subsidies [F009]. Honesty requires the risk memo: France's last reactor came in four times over budget and twelve years late [F052]. So every bet in this program, nuclear included, is born with a published kill criterion and an independent evaluator. The East Asian states that industrialized fastest did not succeed by betting; everyone bets. They succeeded by culling losers without mercy [F060]. France's failure mode has never been betting. It is refusing to kill.

**Schools.** The slowest lever, therefore the first to start. Teacher pay rebuilt toward German levels, which at mid-career means roughly doubling it [F047]. The math collapse [F022] treated as the national emergency it is. Autonomy traded for accountability, school by school. Education moves national growth with a generation's lag, on estimates serious people still dispute [F034], which is why a thirty-year program begins there and why five-year governments never do.

**People and land.** The expiring demographic card can be partly reshuffled. French families say they want 2.3 children and have 1.6; the gap is not desire but constraint, and the constraints have names: childcare, square meters, security of income [F044]. For a decade France had Europe's strongest natality and its most generous family policy. It trimmed the policy in 2013-2015 and watched births slide from 2015 on [F044]. Causality is debated; the order of events is not. So restore the policy, aimed at the gap. Add a points-based skilled immigration channel, and notice what it actually is. Today one French residence permit in six is granted for work; in Canada, six admissions in ten [F043]. Points are a reform that *increases* control over who comes. And build: housing is the largest line in every young household's budget [F042], the quiet tax on mobility, on family size, on everything this program needs. The pension fund, conveniently, is a natural investor in rental housing.

**The boundaries.** Because this must be said in a French essay: none of the above is privatization. The grids, the rails, the water stay public or regulated; Britain privatized its railways into fragmentation, a tripling of real public subsidy, and a crawl back to full renationalization [F057]. Health stays universal; the American counter-example costs six points of GDP more for five fewer years of life [F055]. What the public sphere owes in return is management. In 2022, the State's civil service, two and a half million people, dismissed thirteen of them for professional insufficiency [F061]. Thirteen. That does not protect public workers; it discredits them. And universality does not require the price tag: Spain spends two points of GDP less than France and its people live a year longer [F058]. Reform inside the public frame. Ownership was never the variable that mattered.

Add it up and what does it buy? I built a small model to answer that, every assumption public, partly to discipline my own enthusiasm. It did. The honest central scenario is not the tripling of growth I wanted to claim. It is a doubling of France's pace: from 0.8 to around 1.5 percent per head per year, sustained for two decades. That would make France the fastest-growing large economy in Europe throughout. It would carry France past Germany's living standard sometime in the 2040s, if Germany stays on its current trend. If Germany recovers, France closes most of the gap without overtaking, and I will take that outcome too. There is one more finding worth stating against my own case: the work and simplification levers exhaust themselves by the late 2030s. What carries the third decade is schools, science and demography. The program is a relay race, and the slow runners start first.

## The objections

Six punchlines chase every French conversation about growth. Open the ones that chase you.

<details>
<summary><em>"You can't have infinite growth on a finite planet."</em></summary>

Concede the cosmology: material throughput cannot grow forever, and the serious debate, decoupling speed against carbon budgets, deserves respect. But three facts sit between that cosmology and the French budget. First, France has already grown by a fifth since 2005 while cutting its emissions by a quarter, imports included; eighteen developed economies have done the same on peer-reviewed counts [F065]. Second, France is the large economy where growth is cleanest at the margin: about 40 grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour, against 210 for the EU and 330 for Germany [F064]. A euro of French growth is the least carbon-costly euro available on this continent. Third, degrowth does not shrink debt. The 3,460 billion stays nominal while the economy beneath it shrinks. The ratio explodes. The forced adjustment arrives early. And it lands hardest on the young and the newly poor, the way Greece's did [F085][F086]. Shrinking on purpose, alone, over-indebted, without global coordination, is not a climate policy. It is the crisis from section two, volunteered for. And the transition itself, reactors, grids, adaptation, is a capital program that only a solvent, growing country can pay for [F032].

</details>

<details>
<summary><em>"Japan lives fine at 250 percent debt. 115 is nothing."</em></summary>

Japan owes yen, prints yen, and owes them overwhelmingly to its own citizens. France owes euros it cannot print, and 54.6 percent of its government debt sits in foreign hands [F028]. Both legs of the comparison fail. A better comparison for a euro member that lost market confidence exists, and its name is Greece.

</details>

<details>
<summary><em>"Then leave the euro and print francs."</em></summary>

Take the exit seriously, because the law allows it. Nearly all French government debt is written under French law; parliament could vote it into francs tomorrow [F088]. Everything after that vote is arithmetic. Redenominating into a currency built to fall counts as a default under the rating agencies' published definitions [F089]. The party proposing the new franc assumed it would drop about twenty percent [F089]. The fullest published costing, Institut Montaigne's in 2017, found 2.3 points of GDP gone in the first year, and half a million jobs [F089]. And the presses cannot print what France buys abroad. Its energy arrives priced in dollars, 116 billion euros of it in 2022 [F091]. Its savers hold 1,571 billion euros of life insurance that would convert and sink with the franc [F023]. Greece only approached the door: 48 billion in deposits fled in six months; then the banks closed for three weeks, and the cash machines gave sixty euros a day [F090]. One detail finishes the argument. Both French parties that proposed exit have quietly buried it. The Rassemblement National dropped the franc in 2019, and the reporting at the time gave the reason: the idea terrified retirees, the voters who hold the wealth [F092][F078]. When Mélenchon renounced it in 2022, he named this essay's own reason, the external deficit [F092].

</details>

<details>
<summary><em>"The ECB will never let France fail."</em></summary>

Probably true, and beside the point. The ECB's crisis instrument is explicitly conditional: it protects countries that are executing a fiscal adjustment. It buys time for a program; it is not a substitute for one. Ask Athens what the protected version of failure feels like. The euro removes the currency crisis and replaces it with something quieter: the negotiated loss of budgetary sovereignty. The backstop decides how France adjusts. It does not decide whether.

</details>

<details>
<summary><em>"Inflation will take care of the debt. It already did in 2022."</em></summary>

It did, briefly. The 2021-2023 price surge shaved about five points off the debt ratio [F074]. Then watch what happened next. French pensions and the minimum wage are indexed by law, so spending reflated within a year. One euro in nine of the state's debt is inflation-linked and repriced instantly [F074]. The rest rolls over at 300 billion a year, at whatever rate an inflation surprise produces [F029]. By 2025 the ratio was climbing again, past where it had started [F002]. Inflation turned out to be a one-off levy on creditors, re-spent in real time. And the lever sits in Frankfurt anyway. France can no more choose inflation than it can print the euros it owes.

</details>

<details>
<summary><em>"Just tax wealth instead."</em></summary>

Run the numbers, because they are not close. The old wealth tax raised about five billion a year in its final years [F050]. The most ambitious current proposal is a two-percent floor on fortunes above a hundred million. Its own author scores it at twenty billion a year; economists using the state's best estimates of avoidance and exit get five [F050]. The program above needs on the order of a hundred billion a year in recovered margin. Take whichever score you prefer; the hole swallows both. More to the point, no wealth tax adds a single hour of work or a point of productivity. As a fairness signal inside the program, fine; some version may even be good politics. As an alternative to the program, the arithmetic laughs.

</details>

One objection gets no collapsible, because it is this essay's own spine turned against it: the impasse. If the older majority cannot be outvoted, why would it ever vote for any of this? Three honest, partial answers, all of them already built into the design. Current pensions are grandfathered; the reform bites flows, not stocks, which splits the losing coalition. The fund reaches fifty-year-olds too, and with fifteen working years left to compound they are among its biggest winners. And the holders of the alternative, the imposed version, are their own children, a fact no French grandparent enjoys having spelled out. I will not pretend this adds up to a majority this morning. It adds up to a majority the day after the crisis starts, which is the day the program is written for.

## How it actually happens

Because none of the above passes today's National Assembly, and I know it.

Here is what Sweden did with the same problem. In December 1992, with the krona broken and rates at absurd heights, the finance minister asked an economist named Assar Lindbeck to write down everything that needed to change. His commission delivered 113 proposals in three months. Governments of the left and the right then spent a decade implementing them, taking turns [F040]. The plan preceded the window. When the window opened, Sweden did not spend it debating. It spent it signing.

That is the entire strategy, transposed. Phase zero, now, before any crisis: everything that needs no parliamentary majority. Auto-enrollment defaults on the existing retirement savings plans. Employee shareholding expanded by decree. Permitting deadlines. The teacher-pay trajectory in the next budget. The kill-criteria discipline on every industrial bet already running. And above all: the full program, written, costed, published, sitting on the shelf in plain view. Not a manifesto. An operating manual with the screws numbered.

Then the window. Maybe it opens with a failed auction, maybe with a downgrade too far, maybe merely with a government that arrives at the Élysée the morning the interest line crosses the education budget. Windows like it opened for Sweden in 1992 and for Greece in 2010, and the difference between those two stories, sovereignty kept versus sovereignty rented out, was not courage or culture. It was whose plan was on the table when the room started shouting.

And in the first phase of execution, one rule above all the others: winners first. Net wages rise at the bottom in year one, because the tax cliffs go first. The fund statements start arriving in year two, an envelope for every worker in France, each with a number that grows. The costs, the retirement age, the indexation discipline, phase in behind the gains, not ahead of them. Reforms die when their winners are hypothetical and their losers are organized. So the program manufactures its winners early, visibly, by name and by account number, and lets them defend the rest.

## The choice

Two mornings, then.

On the first morning, the auctions have gone quiet and the men from the institutions are in the conference room. The program France executes is written by people who do not have to live here. It is optimized for creditor recovery, on a creditor's calendar. Investment dies first, because it is the easiest line to cut; Greece's fell by two thirds in two years [F087]. Then the safety net fails the new poor it was never built to catch. In Greece, poverty measured against the pre-crisis line went from 18 to 48 percent of the population in four years [F085]. The young pay longest: sixty percent youth unemployment, 427,000 educated emigrants [F086]. And the pensioners, through all of it, kept their relative place [F086]. A forced adjustment does not break the machine this essay has described. It runs the machine at creditor speed, against the same children. Everything still happens, but faster, dumber, and to France instead of by it.

On the second morning, none of that has occurred. A few years earlier, the country did the strangest thing in its modern history. It read its own books. It named the trade it had been running for fifty years: comfort now, paid by the children. And it renegotiated the terms itself. It worked more and owned more. It pointed its rivers of sleeping savings at its own future. It powered a continent's intelligence with its reactors. It taught its children mathematics like it meant it. And it kept the one promise that makes it France, because it could finally afford to keep it: that nobody here dies for lack of money.

Both mornings close the ledger this essay opened with; the three euros of debt per euro of growth stop either way. The only question left, and it has been the only question since the first page, is the handwriting on the budget.

France still holds the pen. Not for long. But it holds it this morning, and this morning is when programs get written.
